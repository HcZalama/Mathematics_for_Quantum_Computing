\chapter*{Conclusions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusions}
\markboth{Conclusions}{Conclusions}

We began with three experiments that classical physics could not explain and with four rules, stated without proof, that resolve them. Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} built the language in which the rules are written: complex amplitudes, vector spaces, inner products, operators and their spectra, and tensor products. Chapter~\ref{ch:postulates} stated the rules precisely and drew their consequences, and Chapter~\ref{ch:info} gave the probabilities they produce a quantitative theory. Chapter~\ref{ch:phenomena} then came back to the three experiments. The fringes of the double slit are the interference of two amplitudes, and they fade exactly as far as the path is recorded somewhere (Section~\ref{sec:interference}). Atoms have sharp spectral lines because energies are eigenvalues and only their differences are ever seen, and they do not collapse because confinement costs kinetic energy (Section~\ref{sec:levels}). And the black-body spectrum is the state of largest entropy for light whose energy comes in whole quanta (Section~\ref{sec:blackbody}).

Four ideas deserve to be remembered above the rest.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{States are rays and phases matter relatively.} A global phase cannot be seen; a relative phase can. This is why interference exists, why a superposition of energy levels moves while a single level does not, and why quantum algorithms are written as arrangements of phases.
  \item \textbf{Evolution is reversible; measurement is not.} Unitaries preserve information; measurements extract some of it and disturb the rest. That is why a state cannot be copied (Section~\ref{sec:teleportation}), why a measurement of $S_x$ erases what was known about $S_z$ (Section~\ref{sec:spin}), and why a quantum algorithm is a clever sequence of unitaries ending with a measurement.
  \item \textbf{Entanglement is a resource.} It makes the parts of a system less definite than the whole (Section~\ref{sec:density}), it lets one qubit carry two bits (Section~\ref{sec:superdense}) and two bits carry one qubit (Section~\ref{sec:teleportation}), and it cannot be reproduced by any local, realistic theory (Section~\ref{sec:bell}).
  \item \textbf{Knowledge has a number.} Every measurement outcome is a random variable, and the entropies of Chapter~\ref{ch:info} measure what we do not know: the Shannon entropy of a distribution, the von Neumann entropy of a state, and the relative entropy that tells two of them apart. The same number measures the entanglement of a pure state, and its maximum at fixed energy is the thermal state of Section~\ref{sec:blackbody}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What comes next.}
The tools of this book are the starting point of the rest of the programme. Gates and circuits, the algorithms of Deutsch--Jozsa, Grover and Shor, quantum cryptography and quantum error correction all consist of unitaries, measurements and entangled states, and Fourier analysis, symmetry and entropy return in every one of them. Each of these topics will feel concrete if the material of these chapters feels familiar.

\paragraph{Open questions.}
Some of the questions touched upon here are open, and are worth keeping in mind. Why does measurement collapse the state, and can Postulate~3 be derived from Postulate~2, perhaps through the decoherence of Section~\ref{sec:interference}? What exactly does the violation of Bell's inequality tell us about locality and realism, and which of the two should be given up? And how much entanglement, and of which kind, is needed for a quantum computer to outperform a classical one? Nature, it seems, still has a few surprises left for those who go on asking.
