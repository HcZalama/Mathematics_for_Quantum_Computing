\markboth{Introduction}{Introduction}

This book accompanies two courses, \emph{Mathematics for Quantum Computing I} and \emph{II}, and is accordingly divided into two parts. Part~I is devoted to the first course. Part~II, which is the part
contained in this volume, covers the mathematics that a student needs in order to read quantum mechanics and quantum computing, and the postulates of quantum mechanics themselves.

\paragraph{What Part~II contains.}
It has two chapters. Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}, \emph{Mathematical Tools}, begins with the physical puzzles that forced quantum mechanics upon us and observes that Schr\"odinger's equation is linear.
It then builds the language in which that observation is made precise: complex numbers, vector spaces and linear maps, inner products and Dirac notation, and operators with their spectra.
Chapter~\ref{ch:postulates}, \emph{The Postulates of Quantum Mechanics}, states the four postulates (state space, evolution, measurement, composition), draws their consequences, and goes on to
superdense coding, density operators, the Schmidt decomposition and the argument of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen together with Bell's reply to it.
Figure~\ref{fig:roadmap} shows how the sections depend on each other.

\paragraph{What you need.}
A first course in calculus and linear algebra. Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} reviews everything else, but it does so quickly, so if complex numbers or bases are unfamiliar, read it slowly and do the exercises.

\paragraph{How the book is laid out.}
Each section opens with a short statement of what you will learn, builds the ideas through definitions, results and worked examples, and closes with exercises. The kinds of content have a consistent visual identity, so that you can always tell at a glance what you are looking at.

\paragraph{Conventions.}
Vectors are written as kets $\ket\psi$ (Dirac notation, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:hilbert}). The inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle=\braket\cdot\cdot$ is conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in the second.
A \emph{state} is always a unit vector. The imaginary unit is $\ii$ and Planck's constant is $\hbar$. Appendix~\ref{app:notation} collects the notation, and Appendix~\ref{app:reference} lists the states, matrices and gates that appear most often.

Part~II is written to be read on its own, with every definition it needs restated, so that students of the second course can start here.
