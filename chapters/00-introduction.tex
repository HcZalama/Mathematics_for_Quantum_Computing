\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\markboth{Introduction}{Introduction}

This book covers the mathematics that a student needs in order to read quantum mechanics and quantum computing, together with the postulates of quantum mechanics themselves and the probability and information theory that make them quantitative.

\paragraph{What the book contains.}
It has three chapters. Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}, \emph{Mathematical Tools}, begins with the physical puzzles that forced quantum mechanics upon us and observes that Schr\"odinger's equation is linear.
It then builds the language in which that observation is made precise: complex numbers, vector spaces and linear maps, inner products with Dirac notation, the dual space and the Riesz representation theorem, operators with their spectra, and tensor products.
Chapter~\ref{ch:postulates}, \emph{The Postulates of Quantum Mechanics}, states the four postulates (state space, evolution, measurement, composition), draws their consequences, and goes on to superdense coding, density operators, the Schmidt decomposition and the argument of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen together with Bell's reply to it. Along the way it introduces positive operators, unitary representations of groups, and the operator version of the Riesz theorem.
Chapter~\ref{ch:info}, \emph{Probability and Information}, reviews probability measures and Fourier transforms, and then develops the Shannon entropy, the von Neumann entropy and the relative entropy, which measure what we know and do not know about a state.

\paragraph{What you need.}
A first course in calculus and linear algebra. Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} reviews everything else, but it does so quickly, so if complex numbers or bases are unfamiliar, read it slowly and do the exercises. Section~\ref{sec:prob} assumes nothing about measure theory beyond what it explains.

\paragraph{How the book is laid out.}
Each section opens with a short statement of what you will learn, builds the ideas through definitions, results and worked examples, and closes with exercises. Proofs and solutions are written as short chains of steps, so that each line has a single job. The kinds of content have a consistent visual identity, so that you can always tell at a glance what you are looking at.

\paragraph{Conventions.}
Vectors are written as kets $\ket\psi$ (Dirac notation, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:hilbert}). The inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle=\braket\cdot\cdot$ is conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in the second. A \emph{state} is always a unit vector. The imaginary unit is $\ii$ and Planck's constant is $\hbar$. Logarithms in Chapter~\ref{ch:info} are in base $2$ unless written $\ln$.
Appendix~\ref{app:notation} collects the notation, and Appendix~\ref{app:reference} lists the states, matrices and gates that appear most often.

The book is written to be read on its own, with every definition it needs restated.
